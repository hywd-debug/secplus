Exam-day strategy

  • Skip PBQs first. They're at the start and eat time. Flag them, do all the multiple-choice, then come back. Budget ~1 min per MCQ, leaving 20–25 min for PBQs.
  • Partial credit is generally believed to exist on PBQs. Never leave one blank.
  • Look for the qualifier: BEST, FIRST, MOST, LEAST. Several answers may be true; pick the one that answers the question as asked.
  • "FIRST" in an incident question → follow the IR order. You don't eradicate before you contain.
  • Eliminate two wrong answers, then compare the last two against the exact wording.
  • Choose the CompTIA answer, not the real-world-at-your-job answer.
  • Flag and move on if you're stuck for more than 90 seconds.
  • Bring two forms of ID if testing in-center; for online (OnVUE), clear your desk and run the system test the day before.

1.2 Fundamental concepts

  • CIA triad: Confidentiality (encryption, access control), Integrity (hashing, digital signatures), Availability (redundancy, backups, DDoS protection).
  • Non-repudiation: sender can't deny sending; achieved with digital signatures (private key signs).
  • AAA: Authentication (prove identity), Authorization (what you can do), Accounting (logging what you did). Authenticating people vs. systems (device certificates).
  • Authorization models: see Domain 4.6 (DAC, MAC, RBAC, ABAC, rule-based).
  • Gap analysis: compare current state to a desired framework/baseline and list what's missing.
  • Zero Trust: never trust, always verify.
    Control plane: Adaptive identity, Threat scope reduction, Policy-driven access control, Policy Engine (decides), Policy Administrator (sends the decision/creates session).
    Data plane: Implicit trust zones, Subject/System, Policy Enforcement Point (PEP) (gatekeeper that allows/blocks traffic).
  • Physical security: bollards, access control vestibule (mantrap — prevents tailgating), fencing, video surveillance, guards, access badges, lighting, sensors (infrared, pressure, microwave, ultrasonic).
  • Deception & disruption: honeypot (fake system), honeynet (network of honeypots), honeyfile (bait file), honeytoken (fake credential/data that alerts when used).

1.3 Change management

  • Business processes: approval process, ownership, stakeholders, impact analysis, test results, backout plan, maintenance window, standard operating procedure.
  • Technical implications: allow/deny lists, restricted activities, downtime, service/application restarts, legacy applications, dependencies.
  • Documentation: update diagrams, policies/procedures, version control.

"What must be in place before the change if it fails?" → Backout plan. "Who approves?" → Change Advisory Board (CAB).

1.4 Cryptographic solutions

ConceptKnow this
SymmetricOne shared key; fast; bulk data. AES (128/192/256), 3DES (legacy), ChaCha20. Problem = key distribution.
AsymmetricPublic/private key pair; slow; key exchange & signatures. RSA, ECC (smaller keys, mobile/IoT), Diffie-Hellman (key exchange), DSA.
Encrypt for confidentialityEncrypt with recipient's public key; recipient decrypts with their private key.
Digital signatureHash the message, encrypt hash with sender's private key; verify with sender's public key → integrity + authentication + non-repudiation.
HashingOne-way, fixed length. SHA-256/SHA-3 good; MD5/SHA-1 broken (collisions). Used for integrity & password storage.
SaltingRandom value added to password before hashing → defeats rainbow tables.
Key stretchingHash many times to slow brute force: PBKDF2, bcrypt, Argon2.
Key exchangeDiffie-Hellman/ECDHE; out-of-band exchange. Perfect Forward Secrecy = ephemeral keys; one compromised key doesn't expose past sessions.
Encryption levelsFull-disk (BitLocker/FileVault), partition, volume, file, database, record; transport (TLS, IPsec).
ObfuscationSteganography (hide data in image/audio), tokenization (replace data with token, mapping kept in vault — payment cards), data masking (show ****1234).
HardwareTPM = chip on motherboard (disk keys, secure boot measurement). HSM = dedicated device/appliance for managing many keys (CAs, banks). Secure enclave = isolated processor area. KMS = key management service (cloud).
BlockchainDistributed, immutable open public ledger.

PKI and certificates

  • CA issues/signs certs; RA verifies identity; Root of trust; intermediate CAs form a chain of trust.
  • CSR = Certificate Signing Request (you generate key pair, send CSR with public key to CA).
  • CRL = list of revoked certs (downloaded). OCSP = real-time status check. OCSP stapling = server attaches signed OCSP response, reducing load on CA.
  • Wildcard cert: *.example.com (one level of subdomains). SAN cert: multiple names in one cert.
  • Self-signed: not trusted by default; internal/testing. Third-party: public CA.
  • Key escrow: third party holds a copy of keys for recovery.
  • Certificate pinning: app only accepts a specific cert/public key.
  • Formats: .pem/.crt (Base64), .der (binary), .pfx/.p12 (includes private key), .p7b (chain, no private key).

Attributes: internal vs. external, resources/funding, level of sophistication. Motivations: data exfiltration, espionage, service disruption, blackmail, financial gain, philosophical/political, ethical, revenge, disruption/chaos, war.

2.2 Threat vectors and attack surfaces

  • Message-based: email, SMS (smishing), instant messaging.
  • Image-based, file-based, voice call (vishing), removable device (USB drops).
  • Vulnerable software (client-based vs. agentless), unsupported systems/apps.
  • Unsecure networks: wireless, wired, Bluetooth. Open service ports, default credentials.
  • Supply chain: MSPs, vendors, suppliers.
  • Human vectors / social engineering:
    • Phishing (mass email), spear phishing (targeted), whaling (executives), vishing (voice), smishing (SMS).
    • Pretexting (invented scenario), impersonation, business email compromise (BEC).
    • Watering hole (infect a site the target visits), brand impersonation, typosquatting (gooogle.com).
    • Misinformation/disinformation.

2.3 Vulnerability types

  • Application: memory injection, buffer overflow (write past memory bounds), race condition (incl. TOC/TOU — time-of-check vs time-of-use), malicious update.
  • OS-based, web-based: SQL injection (' OR 1=1 --), XSS (inject script into a page viewed by others; stored vs reflected).
  • Hardware: firmware, end-of-life, legacy.
  • Virtualization: VM escape (break out to hypervisor), resource reuse.
  • Cloud-specific, supply chain (service/hardware/software provider).
  • Cryptographic (weak algorithms), misconfiguration, mobile device (sideloading, jailbreaking/rooting).
  • Zero-day: unknown to vendor, no patch.

2.4 Indicators of malicious activity

Malware

TypeKey trait
RansomwareEncrypts data, demands payment
TrojanDisguised as legitimate software
WormSelf-replicates across network, no user action
VirusNeeds a host file + user action to spread
SpywareMonitors user activity

RootkitHides at kernel/OS level; hard to detect
FilelessRuns in memory/PowerShell; no file on disk
RAT / backdoorRemote control/access

Physical & wireless

Brute force (physical), RFID cloning, environmental. Wireless: evil twin (fake AP with same SSID), rogue AP (unauthorized AP on network), deauthentication, jamming, bluejacking (send unsolicited msgs), bluesnarfing (steal data).

Network attacks

  • DDoS: amplified (small request → big response, e.g., DNS/NTP), reflected (spoofed source = victim).
  • DNS attacks: DNS poisoning/spoofing, domain hijacking.
  • On-path (man-in-the-middle), ARP poisoning, replay (re-send captured traffic/token), credential replay, pass-the-hash.
  • Malicious code.

Application attacks

Injection, buffer overflow, replay, privilege escalation, forgery (CSRF — tricks a logged-in user's browser into sending a request; SSRF — tricks the server into making requests), directory traversal (../../etc/passwd).

Cryptographic & password attacks

Downgrade (force weaker protocol), collision (two inputs same hash), birthday attack. Password spraying (one common password against many accounts — avoids lockout), brute force (all combos against one), dictionary, rainbow tables (precomputed hashes).

Indicators

Account lockout, concurrent session usage, blocked content, impossible travel, resource consumption, resource inaccessibility, out-of-cycle logging, published/documented (data found on dark web), missing logs.

2.5 Mitigation techniques

  • Segmentation (VLANs, subnets), access control (ACLs, permissions), application allow list, isolation/quarantine.
  • Patching, encryption, monitoring, least privilege, configuration enforcement, decommissioning.
  • Hardening: install EDR, host-based firewall, HIPS, disable ports/protocols, change default passwords, remove unnecessary software.

Considerations: availability, resilience, cost, responsiveness, scalability, ease of deployment, risk transference, ease of recovery, patch availability, inability to patch, power, compute.

3.2 Securing enterprise infrastructure

  • Device placement and security zones (DMZ/screened subnet for public-facing servers). Attack surface, connectivity.
  • Failure modes: fail-open (traffic flows if device fails — availability) vs fail-closed (traffic stops — security).
  • Device attribute: active vs passive; inline vs tap/monitor (IPS inline, IDS on tap/SPAN port).
  • Network appliances: jump server (bastion host for admin access), proxy (forward = clients out; reverse = in to servers), IPS/IDS, load balancer, sensors.
  • Port security: 802.1X (port-based NAC; supplicant → authenticator (switch/AP) → authentication server (RADIUS)), EAP.
  • Firewall types: WAF (protects web apps — SQLi/XSS), UTM (all-in-one), NGFW (application-aware, layer 7), layer 4 vs layer 7.
  • Secure communication/access: VPN, remote access, tunneling (TLS, IPsec — AH = integrity/auth only; ESP = encryption; transport vs tunnel mode), SD-WAN, SASE (cloud-delivered network + security).

3.3 Protecting data

  • Data types: regulated, trade secret, intellectual property, legal, financial, human- vs non-human-readable.
  • Classifications: Sensitive, Confidential, Public, Restricted, Private, Critical.
  • States: at rest (disk encryption), in transit (TLS/IPsec), in use (memory; secure enclaves).
  • Data sovereignty (data subject to laws of country where stored), geolocation.
  • Methods: geographic restrictions, encryption, hashing, masking, tokenization, obfuscation, segmentation, permission restrictions.

3.4 Resilience and recovery

  • High availability: load balancing (active/active) vs clustering (active/passive).
  • Site considerations: Hot site (fully ready, fastest, most expensive), warm site (some equipment, needs data), cold site (space and power only, cheapest/slowest). Geographic dispersion.
  • Platform diversity, multi-cloud.
  • Continuity of operations, capacity planning (people, technology, infrastructure).
  • Testing: tabletop (discussion), fail over, simulation, parallel processing.
  • Backups: onsite/offsite, frequency, encryption, snapshots, recovery, replication, journaling.
    Full (everything; clears archive bit) · Incremental (since last backup of any kind; fast backup, slow restore = full + every incremental) · Differential (since last full; restore = full + latest differential).
  • Power: generators (long-term), UPS (short-term bridge).

4.0 Security Operations 28% — biggest domain

4.1 Secure computing resources

  • Secure baselines: establish, deploy, maintain (e.g., CIS Benchmarks, Group Policy).
  • Hardening targets: mobile, workstations, switches, routers, cloud, servers, ICS/SCADA, embedded, RTOS, IoT.
  • Wireless: site surveys, heat maps. WPA3 (SAE replaces PSK handshake — resists offline dictionary attacks), WPA2-Enterprise / WPA3-Enterprise use RADIUS/802.1X. Avoid WEP/WPS.
  • Mobile: MDM. Deployment models: BYOD (user owns), COPE (corporate-owned, personally enabled), CYOD (choose from approved list). Connection: cellular, Wi-Fi, Bluetooth.
  • Application security: input validation, secure cookies, static code analysis (SAST), code signing, sandboxing.

4.2 Asset management

Acquisition/procurement → assignment/accounting (ownership, classification) → monitoring/asset tracking (inventory, enumeration) → disposal/decommissioning: sanitization (overwrite/wipe), destruction (shred, degauss, incinerate), certification (certificate of destruction), data retention.

4.3 Vulnerability management

  • Identification: vulnerability scan, application security (SAST, DAST, package monitoring), threat feeds (OSINT, proprietary, ISACs, dark web), penetration testing, bug bounty, system/process audit.
  • Analysis: confirm — false positive (alert, no real issue) vs false negative (real issue missed — worse). Prioritize with CVSS (0–10 severity score) and CVE (the ID of a specific vuln). Classification, exposure factor, environmental variables, industry/organizational impact, risk tolerance.
  • Response/remediation: patching, insurance, segmentation, compensating controls, exceptions and exemptions.
  • Validation: rescan, audit, verification. Then reporting.
  • Credentialed scans see more (and fewer false positives) than non-credentialed. Non-intrusive vs intrusive.

4.4 Alerting and monitoring

  • Monitor systems, applications, infrastructure. Activities: log aggregation, alerting, scanning, reporting, archiving, alert response (quarantine, alert tuning).
  • Tools: SCAP (standards for automated vuln/config checks), benchmarks, agents/agentless, SIEM (correlates logs from many sources, alerts), antivirus, DLP, SNMP traps, NetFlow (traffic metadata/flows), vulnerability scanners.

4.5 Modifying enterprise capabilities

  • Firewall: rules, access lists, ports/protocols, screened subnets. Rules processed top-down, first match wins; end with implicit deny.
  • IDS/IPS: trends, signatures (known patterns) vs anomaly/behavior-based (deviation from baseline — catches zero-days, more false positives).
  • Web filter: agent-based, centralized proxy, URL scanning, content categorization, block rules, reputation.
  • OS security: Group Policy, SELinux.
  • Secure protocols: replace Telnet → SSH, HTTP → HTTPS, FTP → SFTP/FTPS, SNMPv1/2 → SNMPv3, LDAP → LDAPS.
  • DNS filtering; email security: DMARC (policy for failed checks + reporting), DKIM (digital signature in headers proves message not altered/from domain), SPF (DNS TXT list of servers allowed to send for domain), gateway.
  • File integrity monitoring (FIM), DLP, NAC, EDR/XDR, user behavior analytics (UBA).

4.6 Identity and access management

  • Provisioning/de-provisioning, permission assignment, identity proofing, federation (trust between orgs), SSO.
  • SSO protocols: LDAP (directory), OAuth (authorization — delegated access via tokens), SAML (XML assertions; IdP ↔ SP; web SSO), OpenID Connect (authentication layer on OAuth). Kerberos (tickets, AD, port 88).
  • Interoperability, attestation.
  • Access control models:
    MAC (Mandatory)Labels/clearances set by admin (Top Secret). Military. Most strict.
    DAC (Discretionary)Owner decides who gets access (file share permissions).
    RBAC (Role-based)Access by job role/group.
    Rule-basedSystem rules (e.g., firewall ACL, time-of-day).
    ABAC (Attribute-based)Many attributes: user, location, device, time. Most granular.
  • Time-of-day restrictions, least privilege.
  • MFA factors: something you know (password, PIN), have (token, smart card, phone), are (biometrics), somewhere you are (location). Two passwords = still single-factor! Implementations: biometrics, hard/soft tokens (TOTP time-based, HOTP counter-based), security keys (FIDO2/passkeys).
  • Biometrics: FAR (false acceptance — security risk), FRR (false rejection — usability), CER (crossover — lower is better).
  • Password concepts: length, complexity, reuse, expiration, age; password managers, passwordless.
  • Privileged access management (PAM): just-in-time permissions, password vaulting, ephemeral credentials.

4.7 Automation and orchestration

Use cases: user/resource provisioning, guard rails, security groups, ticket creation, escalation, enabling/disabling services, CI/CD testing, API integrations. SOAR automates response via playbooks. Benefits: efficiency, enforcing baselines, standard configs, scaling, employee retention, reaction time, workforce multiplier. Considerations: complexity, cost, single point of failure, technical debt, ongoing supportability.

4.8 Incident response

Process (CompTIA order): Preparation → Detection → Analysis → Containment → Eradication → Recovery → Lessons Learned
  • Training and testing: tabletop exercise, simulation.
  • Root cause analysis, threat hunting (proactively searching for undetected threats).
  • Digital forensics: legal hold (preserve data for litigation), chain of custody (document who handled evidence when), acquisition, reporting, preservation, e-discovery.
  • Order of volatility (collect most volatile first): CPU registers/cache → RAM → swap/page file, temp files → disk → remote logs → archival/backup media.
  • Take hashes of evidence/images to prove integrity; work from a copy (bit-by-bit image).

4.9 Data sources for investigations

Log data: firewall, application, endpoint, OS-specific security logs, IPS/IDS, network, metadata. Other sources: vulnerability scans, automated reports, dashboards, packet captures (full content — most detail).

5.0 Security Program Management & Oversight 20%

5.1 Security governance

  • Guidelines (recommended), policies (high-level mandatory: AUP, information security, BC, DR, incident response, SDLC, change management), standards (specific mandatory: password, access control, physical, encryption), procedures (step-by-step: change management, onboarding/offboarding, playbooks).
  • External considerations: regulatory, legal, industry, local/regional, national, global.
  • Monitoring and revision. Types of governance structures: boards, committees, government entities, centralized/decentralized.
  • Roles: Owner (accountable, senior person; sets classification), Controller (decides why/how personal data is processed), Processor (processes on controller's behalf), Custodian/steward (day-to-day handling, backups, implements controls).

5.2 Risk management

  • Identification → assessment → analysis → register → tolerance/appetite → strategies → reporting.
  • Assessment types: ad hoc, recurring, one-time, continuous.
  • Qualitative (high/medium/low, heat map) vs quantitative (dollars).
Quantitative formulas
SLE (Single Loss Expectancy) = AV (Asset Value) × EF (Exposure Factor %)
ALE (Annualized Loss Expectancy) = SLE × ARO (Annualized Rate of Occurrence)
Example: $200,000 server, 25% damaged per flood → SLE = $50,000. Flood every 4 years → ARO = 0.25 → ALE = $12,500.
If a control costs more per year than the ALE it reduces, it isn't cost-justified.
  • Risk register: key risk indicators, risk owners, risk threshold.
  • Risk appetite: expansionary, conservative, neutral. Risk tolerance.
  • Strategies: Transfer (insurance, outsourcing), Accept (with exemption/exception), Avoid (stop the activity), Mitigate (apply controls).
  • Business impact analysis (BIA):
    • RTO — Recovery Time Objective: max time to restore.
    • RPO — Recovery Point Objective: max acceptable data loss (time since last good backup).
    • MTTR — Mean Time To Repair. MTBF — Mean Time Between Failures (reliability).

5.3 Third-party risk

  • Vendor assessment: penetration testing, right-to-audit clause, evidence of internal audits, independent assessments, supply chain analysis.
  • Vendor selection: due diligence, conflict of interest. Vendor monitoring, questionnaires, rules of engagement.
  • Agreement types:
    SLAService Level Agreement — performance/uptime metrics
    MOA / MOUMemorandum of agreement/understanding — intent to work together (MOU usually not legally binding)
    MSAMaster Service Agreement — overall terms for future work
    SOW / WOStatement of Work / Work Order — specific deliverables under an MSA
    NDANon-disclosure — confidentiality
    BPABusiness Partners Agreement — partnership terms, profit sharing

5.4 Compliance

  • Compliance reporting (internal/external). Consequences of non-compliance: fines, sanctions, reputational damage, loss of license, contractual impacts.
  • Compliance monitoring: due diligence/care, attestation and acknowledgement, internal/external, automation.
  • Privacy: legal implications (local/regional, national, global), data subject, controller vs processor, ownership, data inventory and retention, right to be forgotten (GDPR).
  • Know names: GDPR (EU privacy), HIPAA (US health data), PCI DSS (payment cards — industry standard, not a law), SOX (financial reporting), GLBA (financial institutions), FERPA (student records).

5.5 Audits and assessments

  • Attestation; internal (compliance, audit committee, self-assessments); external (regulatory, examinations, assessment, independent third-party audit).
  • Penetration testing: physical, offensive (red), defensive (blue), integrated (purple). Environments: known (white box — full info), partially known (gray box), unknown (black box — no info).
  • Reconnaissance: passive (OSINT, no direct contact) vs active (scanning the target).
  • Rules of engagement define scope, timing, allowed techniques.

5.6 Security awareness

  • Phishing campaigns: recognize attempts, respond to reported suspicious messages.
  • Anomalous behavior recognition: risky, unexpected, unintentional.
  • User guidance and training: policy/handbooks, situational awareness, insider threat, password management, removable media and cables, social engineering, operational security, hybrid/remote work environments.
  • Reporting and monitoring: initial and recurring. Development and execution.
